The 2026 Datathon asks how to make life more affordable in Virginia, and electric bills are one of the fastest-rising costs. Data centers have driven a 60% jump in commercial electricity sales since 2016, residential prices rose 26% from 2022 to 2026, and JLARC projects data-center growth could add $14 to $37 a month to typical Dominion bills by 2040. In the Energy focus area, we asked: which households can least afford these increases, is existing aid reaching them, and where should new relief go first?
We analyzed DOE LEAD Tool data on 466,049 households at or below 150% of the poverty line in all 133 localities, broken out by income, housing type, and heating fuel, plus VDSS Energy Assistance Program (EAP) and SNAP counts, EIA prices, and JLARC's scenarios. We updated energy costs to 2026 prices and stress-tested them under JLARC's 2040 scenarios, calculated the cost of bringing every low-income household's energy bill down to 6% of income, compared EAP's reach to eligible and SNAP households, and ranked localities with 100,000 Monte Carlo simulations so our priorities don't depend on one set of assumptions.
Low-income households spend 14.3% of their income on home energy, versus 2.1% for all households, and 21–26% with oil or propane heat. Closing that gap would cost $749 million a year today and up to $955 million by 2040. Yet EAP paid just $82 million in 2025 and reached only 28% of eligible households (14% in data-center localities), while 276,934 SNAP households, already income-verified by local DSS offices, got no EAP aid. Need is also deeper in rural areas: a $1,947 gap per household versus $1,510 in metro areas, driven by oil/propane heat and mobile homes.
Our solution, the Cloud Dividend, puts about 20% (~$122 million a year) of Virginia's new data-center electricity tax into an Energy Relief Fund, so the industry driving costs up helps cover them. To close the reach gap, VDSS would match SNAP rolls against utility customer files and automatically enroll households in EAP and the Percentage of Income Payment Program, which caps bills at 6% of income, as New York already does. Since their incomes are already verified, no one has to apply; serving half of the 276,934 at the average $415 benefit costs about $58 million a year. To close the rural depth gap, the fund would weatherize or install heat pumps in the 40,021 low-income oil/propane and mobile homes in priority localities, one-fifth a year at 8,000each(64 million a year), expanding HB2 (2026). Upgrades cut costs permanently, paying for themselves in about six years. The fund covers all of the low-case data-center increase in the gap (77 million) and about 60% of the high case (205 million). Since the tax, DSS offices, and HB2 already exist, the state could start with a $10 million pilot in the eight localities that ranked top-20 in at least 80% of our simulations: Portsmouth, Westmoreland, Radford, Dinwiddie, Mecklenburg, Southampton, Hopewell, and Culpeper.
