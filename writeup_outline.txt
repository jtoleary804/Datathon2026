TITLE
=====================================================================
  "Who Pays for the Cloud? Protecting Virginia's lowest-income households from
   data-center-driven electricity costs"
  Hook (1 sentence): The data centers are in Northern Virginia. The heaviest
  energy bills, relative to income, are in Southside, the Northern Neck, and
  Virginia's small cities, and only about 1 in 4 eligible households gets help.

=====================================================================
1. THE PROBLEM  [TH]
=====================================================================
  - VA commercial-sector electricity sales (where data centers are metered) are
    up 60% since 2016; residential use is flat (EIA).
  - VA residential price: 13.34 cents/kWh (2022) -> 16.83 cents (Jan-Jul 2026),
    +26%. SCC approved +$11.24/mo (2026) for Dominion customers (Nov 2025 order).
  - JLARC (2024): data-center growth alone could add $14-$37/month to a typical
    Dominion bill by 2040 (constant dollars).
  - A flat increase isn't flat in its impact. It hits hardest the households
    already spending a large share of income on energy.
  - Key question in one sentence: who can least absorb these increases, does
    current aid reach them, and where should new money go first?

=====================================================================
2. DATA & METHOD  [DA]
=====================================================================
  Data (name them briefly; judges like "well-sourced"):
    - DOE LEAD Tool (ACS microdata calibrated to utility bills): 466,049
      households at or below 150% FPL, broken out by income, tenure, building
      type, building age, and heating fuel, for all 133 localities.
    - VDSS Energy Assistance Program household counts FFY2017-2025 and SNAP
      participation (Jul 2026), both from the Datathon 2026 collection on
      data.virginia.gov.
    - EIA prices/sales, JLARC Rpt. 598 scenarios, USDA ERS county typology.
  Method (3 steps, one line each):
    1) STRESS TEST: update burden to 2026 prices, then apply JLARC's 2040
       adders per kWh to each household type. Electric-heat and inefficient
       homes take bigger hits.
    2) AFFORDABILITY GAP: dollars needed to bring every low-income household's
       energy bill down to 6% of income.
    3) REACH + PRIORITY: EAP households paid vs. eligible (and vs. SNAP
       households), a depth-vs-reach typology, and a Monte Carlo priority
       ranking. 10,000 draws vary the price scenario, income growth,
       eligibility, and index weights, so the ranking doesn't hinge on one
       set of assumptions. [IN]

=====================================================================
3. WHAT WE FOUND  [DA][TH]  (5 bullets max; bold the numbers)
=====================================================================
  F1. Burden is extreme at the bottom. Households at or below 150% FPL spend
      14.3% of income on home energy vs. 2.1% for all households. Oil- and
      propane-heated low-income homes spend 21-26%.
  F2. The gap is large and growing. It costs $749M/yr today to bring every
      low-income household to 6% of income. That rises to $826M-$955M/yr
      under JLARC's 2040 scenarios, about +$459/yr per low-income household
      in the high case. 314K low-income households are already above 10%
      burden, rising to 358K.
  F3. Current aid covers about 11% of the gap. EAP paid $82M in FFY2025 and
      reached about 28% of eligible households. 276,934 SNAP households,
      already income-verified by the same local DSS office, got no EAP
      heating aid.
  F4. Two different problems need two different fixes:
        - Rural localities: deeper need ($1,947 gap per low-income household
          vs. $1,510 in metro areas; more oil/propane heat and mobile homes)
          but higher reach (49%).
        - Metro localities: reach is only 22%. In the major data-center
          localities, EAP serves just 14% of eligible households.
  F5. Robust priorities. These 8 localities rank in the top 20 in at least
      80% of the 10,000 Monte Carlo draws: Portsmouth, Westmoreland, Radford,
      Dinwiddie, Mecklenburg, Southampton, Hopewell, Culpeper.
      (Mecklenburg and Culpeper are themselves data-center markets.)
      Likely priorities (50-80% of draws): Northumberland, Nelson,
      Pittsylvania, Richmond city, Botetourt, Halifax, Martinsville, Madison.

4. OUR SOLUTION: "THE CLOUD DIVIDEND"  [IN][TH]
=====================================================================
  One-line pitch: Set aside about 20% of Virginia's new data-center
  electricity tax (~$600M/yr) as an Energy Relief Fund, and target it with an
  open, annually refreshed priority index.

  Match the fix to the typology (show the map here):
    LEVER 1 - REACH: automatic enrollment (Reach-gap + Double-gap localities)
      - VDSS matches SNAP households against Dominion/APCo customer files and
        enrolls them in EAP and PIPP (PIPP caps electric bills at 6% of
        income, or 10% with electric heat, for households at or below 150%
        FPL).
      - Precedent: New York's statewide utility data-match law; Austin
        Energy's SNAP/Medicaid auto-enrollment.
      - Pool: 277K SNAP households not in EAP. Serving half at the average
        $415 benefit costs about $58M/yr. PIPP credits are funded by the
        utility universal service fee, not the state budget.
    LEVER 2 - DEPTH: weatherization and heat pumps (Depth-gap + Double-gap)
      - 40,021 low-income oil/propane-heated or mobile homes in priority
        localities.
      - LEAD data shows these homes spend about $2,000/yr more than
        comparable electric-heated homes.
      - Doing 1/5 of them per year at about $8,000/home costs about $64M/yr.
      - Stacks on HB2 (2026), which requires Dominion and APCo to upgrade 30%
        of low-income oil/propane households by 2031, plus the RGGI-funded
        DHCD efficiency programs. Our index tells them where to start.
    LEVER 3 - TARGETING: open Energy Burden Priority Index
      - Published on data.virginia.gov and refreshed yearly from EAP, SNAP,
        LEAD, and EIA data.
      - Directs Levers 1-2 and lets the General Assembly track progress.
  Bottom line: about $122M/yr, roughly 20% of the data-center tax. That
  covers most to all of the data-center-driven growth in the gap
  ($77M-$205M/yr). In short, the industry driving the costs funds the
  protection.

=====================================================================
5. FEASIBILITY: HOW IT GETS DONE  [FE]
=====================================================================
  - Every piece already exists:
      - Tax: in effect since 7/1/2026.
      - EAP + PIPP: run by VDSS through local DSS offices.
      - HB2 utility mandate: signed 2026.
      - DHCD weatherization: RGGI rejoined 7/1/2026.
    No new agency is needed.
  - Step 1 (2027 session): budget language earmarking a share of the tax.
    Extend the tax, which currently sunsets 6/30/2028.
  - Step 2: VDSS-utility data-sharing agreement (NY model).
  - Step 3: Pilot in the 8 robust-priority localities. Covers 24K low-income
    households, 16.5K SNAP households not in EAP, and about 4.6K
    oil/propane/mobile homes, for about $11M/yr (about 2% of the tax).
  - Step 4: Scale statewide in FY2029 and measure the change in reach and
    burden with the same index.

6. LIMITATIONS (one sentence) + SOURCES (footer, small font)
=====================================================================
  Limitations:
    - LEAD's base is ACS 2018-22, updated to 2026 prices. Incomes grow at the
      VA median rate, which likely understates low-income burden.
    - JLARC's scenarios are for Dominion (65% of VA residential customers).
    - "SNAP households not in EAP" is an outreach pool, not an exact eligible
      count (SNAP's 200% FPL limit vs. EAP's 130%).
