### Slide 1 · Title: Who Pays for the Cloud?  
**Rayhan** · 0:00–0:17

Virginia is the data-center capital of the world. So who pays for the cloud? We're DRHS Team 2, and our answer is the Cloud Dividend: use the data-center industry's new tax to protect the Virginians who can least afford rising power bills.

### Slide 2 · The trend: Data centers are pushing everyone's power costs up  
**Rayhan** · 0:17–0:49

Here's the trend. Since 2016, commercial electricity sales, where data centers are counted, are up 60 percent, while homes stayed flat. Building for that demand raises costs for everyone. Residential prices rose 26 percent from 2022 to 2026, and JLARC projects data-center growth alone could add 14 to 37 dollars a month to a typical Dominion bill by 2040. So we asked: who can least afford this, is aid reaching them, and where should help go first?

Meanwhile, 276,934 SNAP households, already income-verified by their local DSS office, got no energy aid at all.

### Slide 6 · Our solution: The Cloud Dividend: the industry driving costs helps pay  
**James** · 2:15–3:09

That's the Cloud Dividend. Virginia's new data-center electricity tax raises about 600 million dollars a year. We put 20 percent, 122 million, into an Energy Relief Fund. Lever one fixes reach: VDSS matches SNAP rolls against utility customer files and automatically enrolls households in EAP and the Percentage of Income Payment Program, which caps bills at 6 percent of income. That's 64 million a year, *[cut if late: and the upgrades pay for themselves in about six years.]* Lever three, our open priority index, tells both levers where to go.

## Likely judge questions (≤20-second answers)

**"How did you build this?"** Walk through it in this order (about 30 seconds):

- **Baseline:** DOE LEAD 2022: households by income band, owner/renter, building type and age, and heating fuel, for all 133 localities.
- **2026 update:** Electricity costs × 1.262 (EIA: 13.34¢ → 16.83¢/kWh); incomes and other fuels × 1.158 (Virginia median income growth).
- **2040 stress test:** JLARC's +$14 / +$37 per 1,000 kWh, applied per kWh to each household type, in 2024 constant dollars.
- **Affordability gap:** For every household at or below 150% FPL (PIPP's eligibility line), the energy cost above 6% of income, summed.
- **Reach:** EAP heating households paid in FFY2025 ÷ estimated eligible households (≤130% FPL), cross-checked against SNAP counts (rank correlation 0.86).
- **Typology:** Localities split at the statewide medians of gap per household and EAP reach.
- **Priority:** 100,000 Monte Carlo draws varying the 2040 price adder (1.4–3.7¢/kWh), income growth (×1.10–1.20), eligibility share (0.5–0.7) and the four index weights (Dirichlet 5,5,5,5).

Full code: `energy_burden_analysis.ipynb`. Every dataset is public; links are in `data_sources.txt`.

**Other likely questions:**

- **Why not just give EAP more money?** EAP misses 72% of eligible households. Bigger checks don't reach people who never apply, so auto-enrollment fixes the leak first; weatherization then lowers the bill permanently.
- **The data-center tax sunsets in 2028. Then what?** Step one is extending it with an earmark. The dividend is only 20% of revenue, and the tax is designed to make data centers pay their share, which this does.
- **Doesn't PIPP already cap bills?** Yes, for Dominion and APCo customers, at 6% of income (10% with electric heat), but you have to apply through DSS. Auto-enrollment gets people into it, and PIPP credits are paid by the utility universal service fee, not the state budget.
- **How accurate is LEAD?** It's DOE's ACS microdata calibrated to utility billing data. We updated prices to 2026 and grew incomes at Virginia's median rate, which, if anything, understates low-income burden.
- **Why these eight localities?** We didn't hand-pick weights. Across 100,000 simulations varying prices, incomes, eligibility and weights, these eight stayed top-20 at least 80% of the time.
- **Is JLARC's estimate statewide?** It's modeled for Dominion, which serves 65% of Virginia's residential customers and part of 104 of 133 localities. PJM capacity costs, which rose about tenfold, hit the other utilities too.
- **Privacy concerns with data matching?** In our design the match runs inside VDSS, which already holds SNAP and EAP/PIPP records, and utilities only get back a list of customers to enroll. That's how New York's law works too.
- **Payback of six years, where from?** About $8,000 per home against roughly $1,400 a year in savings (ACEEE's estimate for heat pump plus weatherization); LEAD shows oil/propane homes pay about $2,000 more a year than electric-heated ones.
